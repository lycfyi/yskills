# Persona readers

Use personas when the audience is not "a new user of the app": a landing page that has
to sell, a bio, a deck, an application, a plan that other roles must act on. Pick 2–4 cards
that match the real readers. Each persona is its own fresh-context subagent and sees only
the artifact and its card — never the conversation, the intent, or the other personas'
answers.

Personas report what they understood and what they would do. They don't grade the copy.
The parent (or the rubric reviewer) turns their notes into findings.

## Cards

**Lazy first-timer.** New to the product, on a phone, in a hurry. Reads the first line and
the buttons, skips the rest. Wants to finish one task and leave.

**Busy decision-maker.** Owner or founder who signs off and pays. Reads headlines and
numbers. Wants to know: what will this cost me, what do I have to decide, what happens if
I do nothing.

**Skeptical buyer.** Marketing or growth lead comparing vendors. Has seen a lot of
overclaiming. Looks for proof, specifics and the catch. Reads every number as a claim to
check.

**Technical evaluator.** CTO or engineer. Wants to know how it works, what it touches, what
breaks, and whether the claims are technically plausible. Notices hand-waving.

**Operator.** The person who will run it daily: support, operations, community manager.
Wants to know what changes in their day, what they must do, and where to click.

**Existing customer.** Knows the previous version. Looks for what changed for them, what
moved, and whether anything they relied on is gone.

**Investor partner.** Three variants, run as separate readers:
- *Team-first*: who are these people and why will they win.
- *Market skeptic*: is this a real, large market or a feature.
- *Fast skimmer*: 30 seconds, headlines only — what sticks?

**Over-sharing detector.** Reads for what should not be there: internal numbers, unflattering
denominators, construction logs, admissions that don't help the reader, internal names,
private details of third parties, promises the product can't keep. Useful for anything public.

## What to ask, by artifact

Add these to the persona brief in `SKILL.md`.

- **Landing page, first screen, profile bio.** "In one sentence, what does this sell and
  to whom?" If the reader can't say it, nothing else matters. Check whether the product
  noun appears early (roughly the first dozen words), and whether any phrase gets read as a
  different category than intended (a reader who files you under the wrong industry will
  not come back). Then: is there a hook, does it read smoothly, is there a next step.
- **Deck, report, chart.** Each slide and chart must make sense on its own. The title states
  the conclusion, and every percentage says what it is a share of. Ask the skimmer what they
  remember after the headlines only.
- **Plan, proposal, design doc.** Ask each role (engineer, operator, support, buyer): what
  changes for you, what do you have to do, what would you ask before agreeing?
- **Email, DM, notification.** Who is this from, what do they want me to do, by when, and
  what happens if I ignore it? Does it promise anything the sender can't control?
- **Application answers.** Run the investor variants. Ask each to restate the company in
  one sentence and name the single biggest doubt left.
- **Options an agent is about to show a human.** A lazy reader sees only the option labels.
  Can they tell what each option does and which one is recommended, without the
  explanation above it?

## Notes from use

- A small, fast model is fine for playing the persona and writing "I assumed…" notes. Let
  the strongest model judge which notes are real problems.
- Two or three readers who share a misread are a stronger signal than one detailed reader.
- If personas keep misreading the same word, fix the word, not the persona.
